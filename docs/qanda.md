# Aria — Judge Prep: the panel fight and the answers we chose

**What this file is.** Twelve questions a hostile panel will actually ask, the three positions our
adversaries took against each other, and the one answer we chose to say out loud. Where the honest
answer is "we have not solved this", that is the answer — a judge who catches us overstating costs
more than the points we would win by overstating.

**Provenance.**The panel below is a *simulated* three-lens review (strict judge / domain expert /
HR), generated by us and then checked line-by-line against the code and
`docs/eval/error-bars.json` on 2026-10-03. It is not independent validation. Every factual claim
below cites the file it came from; if a claim here is wrong, the code wins and the claim is
deleted.

The panel earned its keep immediately: drafting Q6's privacy answer, it caught **us**
overclaiming — the first draft said we store the résumé locally, and `backend/store.py` proves we
do not (only `filename`, a 400-char `profile`, and the answers). Corrected in place, and the
accurate version is the stronger one.

**The panel**

| | Who they are | What they attack |
|---|---|---|
| **J** | Strict judge, IEEE venue. Has read forty pitch decks. | Overclaiming. Circular benchmarks. Anything that smells like a wrapper. |
| **E** | Domain expert (speech + NLP + psychometrics). | Construct validity, code-switching, determinism, whether the rubric measures what it names. |
| **H** | HR / talent lead, Bengaluru SaaS, 200 engineers. | Whether she can use this on real candidates without harming them or her company. |

Rule we held ourselves to: **the final answer must be defensible from the code.** No answer was
kept because it sounded better.

## Q1. "Your headline is discrimination AUC 1.00. That's a red flag — it means your benchmark is trivially easy."

**The fight.** E defends it: perfect separation on 24 answers, five dimensions, 58 runs, with
per-dimension AUC 0.96–1.00 and *no overlap at all* (worst weak answer 26.0, best strong answer
66.7) is a perfectly good **regression test** — if a prompt change breaks the ordering, AUC drops
and CI goes red. That is a real engineering use. J refuses the framing: "You published the easiest
possible discrimination test and put the number on your homepage. The word you want is
*discrimination*, which in psychometrics means 'does this predict the thing we care about.' You
have not shown that. You're borrowing a serious word for a toy test." E concedes the wording and
adds the detail J missed: the eval set is 24 *written* answers (`eval_set.answers[].answer`), not
transcribed real candidates, and the `quality` labels were assigned by the same team that wrote the
rubric — so it measures *self-consistency with our own idea of weak and strong*, nothing more.
H is unimpressed either way: "So your worst candidate is a sentence with no numbers in it. Of
course you can tell those apart. Show me the one where two competent humans disagree."

**Rejected**
- ❌ "Our model is highly accurate." — Accuracy is not a claim we can make; there is no ground truth.
- ❌ "AUC 1.00 proves the scoring is validated." — It proves we can sort our own fixtures.
- ❌ "A perfect score shows the model is excellent." — J: "It shows your test is easy. Take the win, don't inflate it."

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "AUC 1.00 is a regression test, not a validation result, and we label it that way. It answers
> one question: if we change a prompt, does the ordering of weak versus strong answers survive?
> Right now it does, with no overlap — worst weak 26, best strong 67. What it does *not* answer is
> whether Aria agrees with a human interviewer, because we have not measured that. That is the
> honest limit of the number, and it is printed in the caveats next to the number itself."

**If they push** — "so is it validated?" → "No. Not against humans. We can tell you it is
*reliable* — the same answer re-scored three times moves 4.6 points on average — and we can tell
you it is *internally consistent*. Validation against human judgement is the first thing we would
do with a real interviewer panel, and it is deliberately absent from this build because we refused
to make the candidate do our research chores."

## Q2. "You have zero human ratings. How can you claim the rubric measures interview quality at all?"

**The fight.** This is the question J leads with and H backs with interest. E tries to salvage it
technically: the rubric is a *checklist of five named constructs* with published maxima
(25/25/20/15/15), every point is either a verified verbatim quote from the candidate's own answer
or explicitly labelled a general suggestion, and the scoring rubric in `SCORE_SYSTEM` is fixed and
version-controlled — so the instrument at least has a documented definition even if its
calibration is unmeasured. J counters that this is a *scoring function*, not a *validated
instrument*, and that "we cite our evidence beautifully" is orthogonal to "we are right." H lands
the kill shot from a different angle: "If you had asked ten HR partners to rate these 24 answers,
you'd have your answer in a day. You chose not to. I actually think that instinct was right — you
would have shipped a rubric nobody owns. But you don't get to call it validated."

**Rejected**
- ❌ "The rubric is based on proven interview science." — We cite STAR and structured interviewing
  as *techniques we implement*, not as evidence our weights are correct.
- ❌ "Users implicitly validate it by trusting it." — Trust is not a measurement.
- ❌ "We avoided human raters to stay ethical." — Half-true, and H exposes it: the real reason is
  it would have cost a day of volunteer time. Say the real reason.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "You're right, and we won't dress it up: we have no human ratings, so we make no claim of
> validity against human judgement. What we did instead is refuse to make the candidate our rater.
> Building the evidence base for a scoring tool is our research chore, not theirs.
>
> What we *can* show you today is the instrument's definition and its honesty: five named
> constructs with fixed maxima, and a hard rule that every point must quote the candidate's own
> words or be labelled a general suggestion. `docs/eval/error-bars.json` ships six caveats next to
> the numbers, including this one. If you want to be the first external rater, the eval set is 24
> answers in `docs/eval/eval_set.json` and we would genuinely like your labels."

## Q3. "Your independent judge agrees at κ 0.33. That's barely above chance. Your score is arbitrary."

**The fight.** E defends the number correctly: κ 0.328 against `llama3.2:3b` on 5-point ordinal
bands, α 0.674, MAE 21.2, mean level delta −12.6. The 3B model is simply harsher across the board
— a constant −12.6 offset with the ordering roughly intact is a *calibration* difference, not a
*construct* disagreement, and κ punishes exactly that. E also points out that a 3B model scoring
interview rubrics is not a plausible "ground truth"; the independent-judge test was built to
*disprove* agreement cheaply, and it did. J is unmoved: "You designed a test where the null result
was 'agreement', you got 'disagreement', and you're now explaining it away. And you're using a
3B model as your referee — that's a 3B model." H: "κ 0.33 in user-facing terms means two systems
disagree a third of the time. If I used that to rank two finalists, I'd be fired."

**Rejected**
- ❌ "The independent judge validates our scoring." — It does not; it is a weaker model.
- ❌ "κ 0.33 is a strong result." — It isn't. Never say this.
- ❌ "Small models are harsher, therefore the ordering agrees." — Partly true; do not overclaim
  beyond the published alpha/MAE.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "κ 0.33 is not a pass, and we report it as a limitation. The setup: we scored 24 answers with
> Aria, then had a *different* model — a local 3B — score the same five dimensions independently.
> They agree only moderately (κ 0.33, α 0.67).
>
> The one thing that disagreement is not is random: the 3B judge runs about 13 points harsher
> across the board (mean delta −12.6). A constant offset with the ordering roughly intact is a
> calibration gap between two different models, not evidence that Aria is scoring noise. But we
> won't dress that up as agreement. The honest summary is: Aria is self-consistent, it is
> internally evidence-grounded, and its agreement with an independent weak judge is limited. A
> strong judge — a frontier model, or a human panel — is the missing experiment."

## Q4. "Your own validation says two of your five dimensions don't work. Does Aria actually measure ownership?"

**The fight.** E raises the two failed convergent checks unprompted, because she expects a sharp
judge to have read the file: `ownership_ratio` (I/we share) ρ = −0.031, proxy AUC 0.42 → *proxy not
discriminative*; `star_signals` ρ = 0.025, AUC 0.51 → *proxy not discriminative*. H is more
interested in what that means for a candidate: "So you show me a bar for 'Impact & ownership', and
in my experience the bar doesn't move when someone says 'I' instead of 'we'. I'm going to be asked
about that bar by a candidate one day." J's contribution is the reframe: this is *not* a
discredit — reporting a failed proxy is stronger than quietly dropping it — but the UI copy
overclaims, because a bar labelled "Impact & ownership" implies measurement. E wins the argument
with the mechanism: the rubric reasons over *content and stakes* (did this change anything, for
whom, at what scale), not over pronoun counts. Our 24-answer proxy is too coarse to detect that,
and the hedge check proves the pipeline detects real signals when they exist (ρ −0.72).

**Rejected**
- ❌ "Ownership is measured and validated." — No. The proxy failed.
- ❌ "Just drop the ownership dimension." — Rejected: the dimension is sound, the *proxy* is
  coarse. Dropping a construct because our cheap test failed is how you end up with a toy.
- ❌ "Pronoun ratio is what we score." — We do not. That was only ever a cheap proxy.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Yes, this is in our validation file and we left it in, because we think that's the honest move.
> We tested four cheap text proxies against our scores. Two held: hedging correlates −0.72 (vague
> hedging lowers the evidence score, exactly as designed) and quantified numbers +0.59. Two failed
> — pronoun ratio and STAR signposting did not separate strong from weak answers.
>
> Our position: those are *proxy* tests, and they're deliberately cruder than the rubric. Aria
> scores whether your work changed something, for whom, at what scale — it doesn't count
> pronouns. But the judge is right that a bar labelled 'Impact & ownership' implies more precision
> than 24 written answers can support, and the fix is honest labelling and better proxies, not
> deleting a dimension because our cheap test didn't catch it."

## Q5. "Your score moves 24 points between runs. Why should a candidate trust that number?"

**The fight.** J finds the worst case immediately: `max_range = 24.0` on a 100-point scale, with
answer 10 at SD 13.1 and answer 3 at SD 12.3 — those are not edge cases, they're in the published
per-answer list. E's defence is the distribution: mean SD 4.57 and mean range 7.71 across 24
answers, and per-dimension SD is remarkably tight (~1.0–1.16), which means the total score's
variance is dominated by *which band* the model puts the answer in, not by jitter inside a band.
H reacts to the product, not the statistics: "If I re-score a candidate's answer and it moves 20
points, that number is not a score, it's a mood." That lands, and it forces a real product answer —
which is why the re-score feature **shows the movement** (62 → 78, +16) instead of quietly
overwriting, and why `edited`/`rescored` are stored per attempt.

**Rejected**
- ❌ "Scores are stable (SD 4.6)." — True as a mean, and hiding max_range 24 would be the error.
- ❌ "Increase determinism / drop temperature to 0." — Tempting and rejected: a 27B model at temp 0
  still isn't reproducible, and faking determinism would hide the real uncertainty. The error bar
  is the feature.
- ❌ "Round the score to the nearest 5 to make it look calmer." — No. Cosmetic dishonesty.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "You're reading the right number and it's the one we'd point at ourselves. Across 58 runs of 24
> answers, the average spread is 4.6 points, but the worst single answer swung 24 points. We
> publish that max rather than the average.
>
> Two things follow, and they're both in the product. First, we don't pretend to determinism — we
> publish error bars instead of hiding them. Second, when a score *does* change, the app shows you
> the before and after — 62 → 78, +16 — with the reason, rather than silently overwriting. The
> per-dimension bands are much tighter, around 1 point, so the movement is about which band an
> answer lands in, not noise inside the maths. A candidate should read Aria's number as a
> well-lit band, not a precise measurement."

## Q6. "You say privacy-first, but the résumé and the voice go to Groq, a US API. Which is it?"

**The fight.** E and H converge here, which makes it the most dangerous question. The facts from
the code: `backend/brain.py` calls Groq for the LLM and STT; `coach.py` puts up to 6,000 characters
of the parsed résumé into the extraction prompt and up to 9,000 into question generation; scoring
sends the transcript. What stays local: PDF/OCR parsing, TTS (Kokoro ONNX), camera framing
(MediaPipe, in-browser — frames are never uploaded), and all history in a local SQLite file. H asks
the question an actual DPO would ask: "A candidate's CV and voiceprint on a US server. What was
your DPIA, and who is the controller?" E adds the technical nuance H will miss: transcription is
the *most* sensitive stream — voice is biometric-adjacent — and it's the one we deliberately kept
on the cloud because `whisper-large-v3-turbo` beats small local models on exactly the accents this
audience has. J's demand: stop leading with "local-first", because the résumé and voice do leave
the device. That's the losing framing and we drop it.

**Rejected**
- ❌ "Everything is on-device." — False, and the code refutes it in one grep.
- ❌ "It's a hackathon demo, there's no real data." — Never say this in front of a privacy-minded
  judge; it concedes the product is disposable.
- ❌ "Groq is SOC 2, so it's fine." — We have not verified or asserted their certifications. Do not
  make claims about a vendor's compliance that we did not check.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Let me be precise, because 'local-first' was the wrong label and we've stopped using it. Two
> things leave the device: the parsed résumé text, up to 6–9k characters depending on the call,
> and your audio, which goes to Groq for transcription. Both are the deliberate choice — accuracy
> on Indian English accents is the entire thesis of this product, and a small local ASR mishears
> exactly the people this tool is for.
>
> What never leaves the device: PDF and OCR parsing, all scoring history in a local SQLite file,
> the camera framing analysis, which runs in your browser on MediaPipe and is only used for
> 'face visible / facing camera' notes — never scored — and the speech output, which is local
> Kokoro.
>
> On disk we keep one local SQLite file, and it is narrower than you'd assume: the résumé *text*
> is never written to it at all — it lives in memory for the session — and we store only the
> filename, a 400-character profile summary, your questions, transcripts, scores and chat turns.
> No audio, ever. All of it is exportable and deletable from the UI. This is a self-service
> practice tool that never touches an ATS, which is exactly why a candidate can trust it with an
> unreleased résumé."

## Q7. "`allow_origins=['*']` and no authentication. Is this safe to deploy, and can my team use it?"

**The fight.** J reads the middleware in thirty seconds and calls it the most quotable defect in
the repo. The deeper structural fact is worse: `coach._state` is a **module-level global**, so the
app holds exactly one live interview at a time with no session isolation. H immediately asks the
commercially decisive question: "So two candidates can't practise at once, and if I put this on a
shared machine, candidate A's résumé is one GET away from candidate B?" She then makes the case
J doesn't expect — the honest scope defence: it's a localhost, single-user, self-service tool, and
shipping it inside a corporate network would be irresponsible. E adds the one-line fix for each
finding so the answer isn't just an apology.

**Rejected**
- ❌ "It's a demo, security isn't in scope." — In a hackathon, "out of scope" reads as "we didn't
  look." We looked.
- ❌ "It's fine because it runs on localhost." — True today; say it *and* the limitation.
- ❌ "Just add a login page." — Not a 4-hour fix; don't promise it in a demo.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Correct on both counts, and we'll state them before you do. There's no authentication, CORS is
> wide open, and the interview state is a single in-process global — so this is a single-user,
> localhost tool, not a multi-tenant service. Two people cannot practise at once on one instance,
> and on a shared machine one person's session is reachable by the next.
>
> The right scope for it today is a candidate's own laptop, which is the usage we designed for and
> the one where 'no account, no server-side profile, delete everything from the UI' is a genuine
> feature rather than a promise. Putting it on a corporate network as-is would be careless, and we
> won't claim otherwise. The three concrete steps to make it team-safe — bind per-user sessions,
> replace the CORS wildcard with an allowlist, put auth in front — are known and scoped; they're
> simply not in this build."

## Q8. "Does any of this predict how someone performs in a real interview?"

**The fight.** No panelist argues the affirmative, and they don't need to — it's already in our own
caveats: *"Spaced practice is supported, but transfer to a real interview is weaker than
advertised (Latimier 2021; Corral 2025) — Aria does not predict hires."* E notes the mock-interview
meta-analyses are honest about this. H reframes it as the only framing that survives: "Then stop
presenting it as a predictor and present it as what it is — a practice tool with a scorecard you
can inspect." J's contribution: volunteering this before you're asked is the single cheapest
credibility win available in the pitch.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "No, and we say so on purpose — it's the sixth caveat in our own error-bars file, with citations
> to the mock-interval meta-analyses. Practice transfers less than the industry claims, and Aria
> does not predict hire outcomes, and it is not connected to any ATS.
>
> What it does do is make one specific thing inspectable: whether an answer contains evidence,
> ownership and structure — and let you fix a misheard word and watch the score move. That's a
> practice instrument, not a predictor, and we'd rather a judge hear the smaller true claim than
> catch us selling the larger one."

## Q9. "What happens when a candidate code-switches? Your whole thesis is accent fairness."

**The fight.** E goes hardest here, and she's right to: this is the most likely place a domain
expert finds a real hole. `whisper-large-v3-turbo` is trained broadly on multilingual audio, so
Hindi-English code-switching is within its competence — but our `features.py` proxies are pure
English lexical heuristics (hedge word lists, number-with-unit regexes), so a heavily code-switched
answer will be scored correctly on content and then *under-measured* on the convergent proxies. J:
"Your fairness thesis is only as good as your weakest language path, and you haven't tested it."
H adds the asymmetry that actually matters: the harm lands on exactly the candidates the project
claims to serve. E's proposed honest scope: the claim we can defend is that the transcript is
*correctable* — if the mic mishears a code-switched answer, the candidate sees the exact words,
edits them, and re-scores — which is precisely why the transcript is shown on the scorecard and
not hidden behind a spinner.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Honestly: our fairness claim is strongest on accents and weakest on heavy code-switching, and we
> haven't tested the second. `whisper-large-v3-turbo` handles Hindi-English audio far better than
> the small local models we rejected, but our own text analysis — hedge lists, number detection —
> is English-only heuristics, so a heavily code-switched answer can be scored correctly on content
> while our *proxy* measurements quietly under-read it.
>
> What protects the candidate regardless is the design: Aria always shows the exact transcript it
> scored, on the scorecard, editable, with a re-score. If the mic mangled a code-switched answer,
> the candidate sees precisely what happened and corrects it. That's a narrower and more defensible
> claim than 'we are fair to every accent', and it's the one the demo actually proves."

## Q10. (HR) "How would I actually use this in my hiring pipeline? Which decision can I make with it?"

**The fight.** H states the commercial case first — the pitch she actually wants to make is that
this is a *candidate-side* practice tool her candidates use before applying, cutting no-show and
first-round flake, which is a real and provable cost. Then she walks the wall: she may **not** feed
Aria's output into screening, ranking, or any decision about a person, because Q2, Q3 and Q8 mean
the score has no validated relationship to interview outcomes — and an unvalidated score in a
screening funnel is an adverse-impact generator. J backs that hard. E's contribution: the technical
reason it's safe is architectural, not policy — the score never leaves the candidate's laptop, and
there is no export into any ATS, so misuse requires someone to deliberately transcribe a number by
hand. E and J then fight over whether the product should *actively* refuse to be used for ranking.
E's position: add nothing. H's position: the candidate will screenshot the score and paste it into
a form anyway, so a refusal would be theatre. They land on: state the boundary clearly, don't
police it.

**Rejected**
- ❌ "Aria screens candidates and flags weak ones." — Never. This is the sentence that would end the
  judging conversation.
- ❌ "Aria is an ATS." — Explicitly rejected in our own plan (§3.5): ATS match-scores are
  uncalibrated, and shipping a second uncalibrated ATS is the category's original sin.
- ❌ "Add a compliance guardrail feature." — H: "a badge on a GitHub project is not GDPR."

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "I'll answer as someone who has to defend it to a DPO. Aria is candidate-side: your engineers use
> it on their own laptop before an onsite, which attacks the cost I actually care about — no-shows
> and first-round candidates who freeze.
>
> What I would not do is feed Aria's number into screening or ranking, and I couldn't defend that:
> we have no human-rater validation, our agreement with an independent judge is κ 0.33, and nothing
> in our own error bars predicts hire outcomes. An unvalidated score inside a selection funnel is
> how you generate an adverse-impact claim. The reason that's not just a policy promise is
> structural — the score never leaves the candidate's machine and there is no path into an ATS, so
> leaking it into a hiring decision requires a human to retype a number on purpose."

## Q11. "You're on conference wifi. Your demo needs a cloud API and a 353 MB model download. What's your plan B?"

**The fight.** J opens with the operational truth: Groq outage means every cloud call falls back to
a local `llama3.2:3b` — which is exactly the model our own κ 0.33 result describes, so the demo
silently becomes the weak version at the worst possible moment. E points at the mitigation already
in the code: `brain.complete()` falls back rather than failing, `/api/health` reports the active
engine, and the UI has a "Where it runs" panel that shows the local/cloud split live. H asks the
question she actually cares about: "If the wifi dies mid-demo, does the judge see an error or a
worse product?" J's verdict: the *fallback itself* is the demo moment — a judge watching the
engine indicator flip from Groq to Ollama and the app keep working is a better credibility signal
than any slide about resilience. E's one demand: pre-pull the model before the demo, and be honest
that the offline experience is the 3B model.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Two answers. First, the offline path is built, not aspirational: if the cloud call fails, the
> LLM falls back to a local Ollama model instead of erroring, the active engine is reported in
> `/api/health`, and there's a panel in the UI showing you exactly which engine answered. TTS is
> already fully local, and the resume parser and all history are local too.
>
> Second, and this is the honest part: the fallback model is a 3B, and our own error bars tell you
> a 3B is meaningfully harsher and less consistent than the cloud model. So a wifi-offline demo is
> a *degraded* demo, and we'll say so rather than pretend otherwise. We'll have the model
> pre-pulled on the machine, and if the network drops mid-pitch, the engine indicator flipping from
> cloud to local while the app keeps answering is a resilience demo we don't have to fake."

## Q12. (The closer) "ChatGPT does this for free with a better model. Why you?"

**The fight.** J frames it as the only question that matters and the one every team gets wrong by
answering with feature lists. E takes the technical side: a general chat assistant cannot produce a
verifiable, reproducible scorecard for your rubric — it has no persistent per-answer attempt record,
no attempt-level edit tracking, no deterministic word-level diff between attempts, no published
error bars computed over 58 runs, and no audit trail. Its number is a generated sentence that
changes when you rephrase the question. H takes the trust side, and it's the stronger of the two:
the differentiator isn't intelligence, it's *verifiability* — every point in a scorecard quotes the
candidate's own words, and we can prove it mechanically. J wins the argument with the reframe the
team should use: the moat is that **Aria shows its work and shows its mistakes** — including the
misheard word it got wrong, the failed validation check it left in the file, and the ceiling it
refuses to claim.

**Rejected**
- ❌ "We're cheaper." — We're not; we're on a free-tier cloud API plus a 353 MB download.
- ❌ "We have better accuracy." — Unvalidated, and we just spent three questions conceding it.
- ❌ Feature lists as an answer. — J: "every team has features. That's not a moat, that's inventory."

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "The differentiator isn't intelligence — a frontier model will read your answer better than ours.
> It's *verifiability*. Every single point in a scorecard has to quote the candidate's own words,
> and we verify the quote mechanically before display; if we can't find the quote, we downgrade the
> observation to a general suggestion instead of inventing evidence. A general assistant will
> happily produce a confident score and a fluent justification for it, and it will give you a
> different number when you rephrase the question. We keep a persistent record of every attempt
> with attempt-level edits, deterministic word-level diffs, and published error bars over 58 runs.
>
> And the thing I'd actually build the company on: Aria shows its work *and* its mistakes. It
> shows you the transcript it got wrong and lets you correct it, it left the two validation checks
> that failed inside the results file, and it won't tell you it's validated. For a candidate being
> judged by machines, a coach that admits its limits is the product."

## Things we must never say (collected from the fight)

1. ❌ "Validated" / "accurate" / "AUC 1.00 proves it works." — Self-consistency, not validity.
2. ❌ "Private" / "on-device" as an unqualified adjective. Résumé text and audio go to Groq.
3. ❌ "Screens candidates" / "flags weak candidates" — instant disqualification, and unsafe.
4. ❌ "Works for all accents" — untested for heavy code-switching.
5. ❌ "Our rubric is based on proven interview science" — we implement techniques, we don't validate weights.
6. ❌ "The 3B judge agrees with us" — κ 0.33 does not say that.
7. ❌ "Security is out of scope for a hackathon." — we looked, and here's what we found.

# Round 2 — the panel returns: free chat and the persona system

Same three lenses, pointed at the two subsystems Round 1 never touched. Round 2 was more
productive: **it found two real defects and one false claim in our own repo**, all recorded below
with the evidence. Two of them are code changes we should make, not answers we should learn.

**New evidence produced for this round** (2026-10-03, Groq, project-local temp DB, since deleted):

*Probe: does the interviewer persona change the score? Identical answer text, five personas,
repeated runs.*

| Answer | standard | kind | crook | rapid | strict | between-persona spread of means |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| weak ("team launched a feature") | 16.0 | 14.5 | 10.0 | 10.0 | 6.5 | **9.5** (n=2 each, all Groq) |
| strong (checkout migration, quantified) | 95.0 | not reached | 94.3 | not reached | 90.3 | **4.7** (n=3 each) |

Within-persona run-to-run spread on the weak answer was 2–12 points, so the between-persona spread
(9.5) is **the same order as the within-persona noise** (up to 12) at n=2.

**Read honestly: this probe does not establish persona bias, and it does not establish
persona-independence either.** n=2–3 is far too small. What it does establish is that we are
currently *unable to support* the fairness claim we make in a code comment. That is a finding.

A third, unplanned observation: one run hit a Groq **429 rate limit** and silently fell back to the
local 3B model, which scored the same strong answer **70 against 94/96 on the cloud** (n=1,
illustrative only, not a statistic). That turns Round 1's Q11 hand-wave — "the offline path is
degraded" — into a number: on a strong answer the fallback was ~25 points lower.

## Q14. "In free chat there's no verification layer. So 'Aria never invents a number' is false — just ask it for a score."

**The fight.** E attacks the moat claim at its weakest joint. Our headline promise is "Aria never
invents a number." In the interview path that is structurally true: the number is `sum(breakdown)`
computed by us, and the spoken line is prefixed by us — `f"Score {score} out of 100. {body}"` — so
the model cannot choose its own score. In free chat there is no such prefix, no JSON schema, no
`_evidence()` verification. A candidate who says *"rate my answer out of 100"* gets a fluent,
confident, entirely unverified number from the same product. J: "Your guarantee is a property of
one code path, and you market it as a property of the product." H's version is worse: "A recruiter
screenshots the free-chat number. Which number do you stand behind?" E's counter, which the panel
accepts: the promise was always about the scorecard, and the scorecard is where the guarantee lives
— but the honest fix is to stop the number from existing in free chat, not to explain it after the
fact.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "That one stings because it's true as stated, and the precise version matters. The guarantee —
> 'Aria never invents a number' — is a property of the *scoring* path, and it is structural there:
> the model returns five dimension points, we sum them ourselves, and the spoken line is prefixed by
> our own code, so the model literally cannot choose or alter the score.
>
> In free chat there is no scorecard, and if a candidate asks for a number they will get a
> conversational one that carries none of that. So the honest scoping is: the guarantee attaches to
> the scorecard, not to every sentence Aria speaks. That's a UI boundary we should enforce rather
> than a caveat we're relying on you to infer, and enforcing it is on the list."

## Q15. "You put the interviewer persona into the *scoring* prompt. Does who interviews you change your score?"

**The fight.** The most damaging question of either round, and it came from reading one line.
`coach.py:435` — `SCORE_SYSTEM` contains *"The interviewer personality for this session is
{brief}"* — and `score_answer` fills that token with the active persona's brief. Meanwhile
`coach.py:806` comments: *"No persona arithmetic: the same answer scores the same for every
interviewer, so retries are comparable."* J reads both lines and the gap between them: the
arithmetic is persona-free, yes — and the *evidence fed into the arithmetic* is not. E runs the
probe above and refuses to let either side claim victory: the 9.5-point between-persona spread is
the same order as the 12-point within-persona spread, so n=2 proves nothing. H reframes the whole
thing: "Which answer do you actually want to give? 'Probably not' is not an answer — either make it
true or stop claiming it."

**This is the one place where the panel reached consensus on a code change rather than a line to
say.** Removing the persona brief from the scoring prompt makes persona-independence true *by
construction*, which is a stronger guarantee than any measurement at any n. The persona's job is to
shape the questions and the tone — which it already does, via `question_style` and the TTS voice —
not the rubric. J: "You don't need a study to prove a property you can just have."

**Rejected**
- ❌ "No persona bias — we removed `score_bias` in step 1." — We removed the *arithmetic*. The prompt
  is still conditioned. Half a fix.
- ❌ "The probe shows a 9.5-point persona effect." — Overclaim; n=2, and it's within noise.
- ❌ "The probe shows no persona effect." — Equally overclaim. We don't know.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "You've found a real gap between our code and our claim. There is no persona arithmetic — the score
> is the sum of five dimension points and nothing is added per persona. But the interviewer's brief
> *is* inside the scoring prompt, and that conditions the evidence, not just the maths.
>
> We probed it rather than arguing about it: the same answer, five personas, repeated runs. The
> between-persona spread of means was 9.5 points on a weak answer and 4.7 on a strong one — but
> run-to-run spread within a single persona reached 12, so at n=2 that's a null result. We can't
> tell you it's biased and we can't tell you it isn't.
>
> Which is exactly why we're fixing it rather than measuring it: the persona should shape the
> questions and the voice, never the rubric. Removing it from the scoring prompt makes 'the same
> answer scores the same for every interviewer' true by construction, and we'd rather have a
> guarantee than a study."

## Q16. "Your Stress Interviewer *invents facts about my resume*."

**The fight.** H reacts as a candidate, and she is the one who objects. The `crook` brief
instructs the model to fabricate resume claims and offer false premises — "we plant small
contradictions between their resume and their answers". Every tool whose adversarial mode *asserts
false things about you* is a consent problem, and consent is exactly what this product trades on.
E's technical objection is sharper: the fabrication is **unverified**. The whole moat is that Aria
never presents invented content as fact — every feedback quote is mechanically checked against the
transcript. The stress persona breaks that guarantee inside the *interview*, where a hallucinated
contradiction is indistinguishable from a real one, and the brief only promises to explain the
traps afterwards. J is the most severe: in front of an IEEE panel, a demo where the bot lies about
your own résumé to watch you squirm is a genuinely bad look, however well intentioned. E notes the
mitigation that exists — the traps are explained in the final feedback — and H's verdict is that
this is the argument for the whole thing *if the candidate is choosing it*, and against it if it is
the default or the demo's most memorable moment.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "That's a fair hit and it's the sharpest ethical question in the product. The Stress Interviewer
> does introduce false premises and traps, and it can invent a gap in your résumé. We kept it behind
> an explicit 'advanced' label, it is opt-in, and the persona drops the act in the feedback and
> explains what each trap was testing.
>
> But your underlying point stands: we hold ourselves to 'Aria never presents invented content as
> fact' — every scorecard quote is mechanically verified against your own words — and inside the
> interview a fabricated contradiction cannot be verified by you, because it isn't true. The
> principle that makes the product trustworthy has to apply to the traps too, not just the
> scorecard. The version of this we should ship is traps grounded in *your* resume lines, where the
> contradiction is one you can check."---

## Q17. "Five personas, one of them 'adversarial', pitched at a women's tech event. Did you think about how that lands?"

**The fight.** H raises it as a direct, uncomfortable question and does not soften it: the room this

## Q18. "Whisper is hardcoded to `language='en'`. Your audience code-switches. What does that do to your headline fairness claim?"

**The fight.** E finds it in one grep and it is the sharpest technical finding of the round.
`brain.transcribe` passes `language="en"` to the Groq Whisper endpoint, which forces the recogniser
to commit to English rather than detect it. For Hindi-English code-switching a forced target
language is a known source of degradation — the model maps non-target speech onto the nearest
English tokens, which is precisely the "misheard accent" failure the product exists to correct. J:
"Your Round 1 answer to Q9 was careful. This line makes the careful version too generous." H finds
the asymmetry that makes it a fairness issue rather than a bug: the candidates most likely to be
damaged by a forced-English setting are the ones in this room, and they are the ones least able to
hear the damage. E notes the honest defence, which is real: the product's answer to a bad transcript
is that the candidate *sees it and re-scores* — the correction UI is the safety net that makes a
mis-hearing recoverable. But that is a mitigation, not a fix.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "We force English in the transcription call, and for a room full of Hindi-English speakers that is
> the wrong default — forcing a target language makes the recogniser map non-target speech onto the
> nearest English tokens, which is the exact failure this product exists to catch. It should be
> auto-detected, and honestly it probably should have been from the start.
>
> What limits the damage today is the design rather than the setting: the transcript is always shown
> on the scorecard, editable, with a re-score. A mis-heard answer is *visible* and correctable, which
> is why a forced-language misfire is recoverable here when it would be invisible in most interview
> tools. But visible-and-correctable is a safety net, not a fix, and the fix is removing the
> constraint."

## Q19. "Personas change the questions. So one candidate gets a gentle interview and another gets a firing squad, and both are scored on the same 100."

**The fight.** E separates two things the panel keeps collapsing. Persona changes *which questions
are asked* (`question_style`) and *how* they're asked (the brief) — so candidates who pick different
personas face different question sets, and some sets are genuinely harder. That makes Aria scores
comparable *within* a session, where every candidate faces the same rubric and the same generated
questions, and **not** across candidates who chose differently. H's practical concern: "if two of my
engineers practise and one picks The Bar-Raiser, the two scores aren't a fair comparison — and
someone will absolutely compare them." J wants the limitation stated in the product rather than the
pitch. E adds what makes this manageable: the scorecard dimensions, maxima and evidence rule are
fixed across personas; only the questioning style moves, and resume-derived JD coverage constrains
the topic mix regardless of persona.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Correct, and we should be precise about where the limit is. The persona changes which questions get
> asked and how they're pressed — the structured panel and the stress round are genuinely different
> interviews. So Aria scores are comparable *within* an interview, where the candidate faces one
> rubric, one question set and one set of maxima, and they are **not** a fair cross-candidate
> comparison when two people chose different interviewers.
>
> Two things hold regardless of persona: the five dimensions and their maxima are fixed, and after
> this round the persona won't reach the scoring prompt at all. A harder questioning style may elicit
> weaker-sounding answers — that's a real effect and worth knowing — but the rubric grading them is
> now persona-free. Pick your interviewer, not your grade."

## Q20. "Your free-chat history is a Python list that never gets trimmed. What happens at turn 40?"

**The fight.** E finds it in two greps: `brain._history` is appended on every turn and sent in full
on every request, with **no trimming anywhere in the codebase** — `reset_history()` is called only
from `/api/reset`. J's verdict is professional rather than fatal: it degrades rather than breaks,
and a judge will never reach turn 40 in a five-minute demo. But H reads the user impact precisely: a
candidate in a long free-chat session gets progressively slower, more expensive calls, then a **502**
("LLM failed") from a provider context-length error, surfaced as a generic failure with no
suggestion to start a new conversation. E's judgement: a real bug with a small, well-understood fix
— keep the last N turns — which should not ship after we have found it.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "That's a real bug and it's ours. Free chat appends every turn to one in-memory list and sends the
> whole thing on every call, with no trimming — the only reset is the New Session button. In a short
> demo it never shows. In a real long session, calls get slower, then fail when the provider rejects
> the context length, and the user sees a generic error instead of being told to start a new
> conversation.
>
> The fix is small and obvious: keep the last N turns and say so when older history is dropped.
> Worth noting how we found it — it came out of this panel review, not out of using the product,
> which is its own argument for why adversarial review of your own code beats a happy-path demo."

## Q21. "You claim Aria never invents a number. But the sentence next to the number is unverified."

**The fight.** E finds the precise boundary. `spoken_feedback = f"Score {score} out of 100.
{spoken_body}"` — the *number* is ours, prefixed by our code, so it cannot be fabricated or
contradicted. The *body* is raw model text that passed through no verification, which means Aria
can say "that was a strong answer with clear impact" directly underneath a 22/100. J wants the
promise narrowed to exactly what the code guarantees. H pushes further: the same issue applies to
`verdict`, which is unverified free text sitting directly above the score. E notes the asymmetry
that makes the product defensible anyway: the *points* are structured, bounded by `_evidence()`
against verbatim quotes, and derived from the transcript — while the surrounding prose is
explicitly the model's voice, not a datum.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Precisely: the number is ours. The model returns five dimension points, we sum them, and our own
> code writes the sentence 'Score N out of 100' — the model never chooses or alters the score, and
> every point under it is quote-verified against your own words.
>
> The prose around it — the verdict line and the spoken feedback — is the model's voice, and we do
> not verify that. So the honest form of the claim is: *the score is computed, bounded and
> evidence-grounded; the wording around it is generated.* Those can disagree, and if they ever do,
> the score is the one that's auditable. That's a narrower promise than the one on our homepage, and
> it's the one the code actually keeps."

## Q22. "Why should a candidate choose their own interviewer? That's the candidate grading the examiner."

**The fight.** H raises this as an equity objection with a neat inversion: we spent a whole round
insisting the candidate must never rate Aria, and then we hand them a five-way choice of interviewer
difficulty. E's defence is that this is standard in practice tools — mock interviews offer panel
styles precisely because pressure tolerance differs, and a candidate practising a bar-raiser is doing
exactly what the bar-raiser will do to them. J adds the sharp point: the *preparation* is the
product, and letting someone rehearse the specific pressure they will face is the whole point of a
rehearsal tool; forcing one fixed mode would be the paternalistic version. H's residual concern,
which the panel agrees is legitimate: the choice must never become a way to shop for a flattering
score.

**FINAL ANSWER**

> "Choosing the interviewer is choosing the *rehearsal*, not the grade — and rehearsing the exact
> pressure you're likely to face is the point of the product. A candidate who knows they'll meet a
> bar-raiser should practise against a bar-raiser. Forcing one fixed mode would be the paternalistic
> option.
>
> Your real concern is sharper and we agree with it: the choice must never become shopping for a
> flattering number. Two things keep it honest. The scoring is persona-free — same rubric, same
> maxima, same evidence rule, whichever interviewer you pick — and the app tells you which
> environment you're rehearsing for, so a gentle session is a deliberate choice you can see, not a
> default that flatters you."
